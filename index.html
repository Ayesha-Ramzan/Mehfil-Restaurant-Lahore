The kitchen

That is the whole point.

We are not recreating old Lahore. We simply never stopped cooking it.

Our Story

A house of slow fires

Mehfil began in a single room on Mall Road with one tandoor, one pot of nihari and a conviction that the old cooking of Lahore — the long, patient, unhurried cooking — deserved a proper table. Not a cart, not a rushed kitchen, but a room where the food could arrive the way it was meant to.

Twelve years on, the pot has never missed a dawn. The suppliers are the same. The mango wood is cut in the same yards. The biryani is still sealed with dough, the champ is still finished over coals, and the first portion of the morning still goes to whoever is sitting at the counter when the shutters rise.

We are a small house. We would rather cook fewer things properly than many things quickly.

The Chef

Ustad Iqbal Hussain

Ustad Iqbal learned nihari at his father's shoulder in a kitchen no wider than a doorway, off the old Anarkali. He has cooked it every morning since he was fourteen. He does not measure with spoons; he measures with steam, with colour, with the sound the pot makes at hour six.

He writes the day's fire on a slate by the pass each afternoon. If it says nalli, there are nalli. When it is gone, he draws a line through it himself.
